# "What If Animals Could Sue Humans" - original script v1 (2026-11-03)
Facts (sources):
- Naruto v. Slater: a crested macaque in Sulawesi, Indonesia pressed the shutter of photographer David Slater's camera (2011). PETA sued in 2015 as the monkey's "next friend"; the 9th Circuit ruled in 2018 that animals lack standing under the Copyright Act. (Wikipedia "Monkey selfie copyright dispute"; 9th Cir. Naruto v. Slater, 888 F.3d 418)
- US Copyright Office: works produced by animals can't be registered (Compendium, 313.2).
- Most legal systems class animals as property ("things"), not persons.
- 1519, Autun, France: rats put on trial for eating barley; lawyer Bartholomew Chassenee defended them and argued they could not safely travel to court because of the town's cats. (Wikipedia "Animal trial")
- About 70+ billion chickens are slaughtered each year (FAO).

A monkey once took a selfie, and it ended up in an American courtroom.
It's Indonesia, 2011. A photographer sets up his camera, and a macaque named Naruto presses the button himself.
The photo goes around the world, and so does the argument: who owns it, the man or the monkey?
An animal rights group sues on the monkey's behalf, and the case climbs all the way to a federal appeals court.
Then the judges say no, an animal can't sue under copyright law.
Luckily, the monkey never had to pay a cent.
Unluckily, he never got paid a cent either.
You see, the law sorts everything into two boxes: people, and things. Animals sit in the things box, right next to the chair.
Humans never minded putting animals on trial, though. In 1519, a French town sued some rats, and their lawyer said they couldn't come to court because of the cats.
So imagine the other way around. Chickens alone would file a class action with seventy billion plaintiffs every year.
And the judge would have to read every single one.
Today, that famous selfie belongs to nobody, because the copyright office says a monkey can't own it, and neither can anyone else.
